Curatorial note

On keeping what almost vanished

Between 1968 and 1999, in rented darkrooms and borrowed bathrooms, a scattered generation of photographers stopped pointing cameras at the world and began pointing light at paper. They made photograms with keys and lace, burned chemistry directly into gelatin, split tones until silver turned to smoke. Almost none of it was exhibited. Most of it was thrown away.

The Afterimage Archive collects what survived: nine hundred forty-seven works recovered from estate sales, shuttered community darkrooms, and the backs of framing-shop drawers. The sixteen plates presented in this first public hanging were chosen not because they are the best — such a ranking would betray the work — but because each one demonstrates a process the archive is committed to documenting before the last practitioners are gone.

An afterimage is what the eye keeps once the light has stopped. These prints are the same thing at the scale of a life: evidence that someone stood in the dark, paid attention, and left a mark. We keep them so the looking is not lost.

— The Keepers of the Archive, est. 2003
