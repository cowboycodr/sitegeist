The ocean, in three voices

These clips are synthesized on your device from the measured pitch contours of real recordings — a faithful sketch of each call, generated locally with no network connection.

  • Humpback whale

    Song unit · 200–600 Hz · Barkley Canyon

    A rising moan that carries for tens of kilometres. Males repeat and slowly remix these phrases across an entire season.

  • Fin whale

    20 Hz pulse train · Ross Ice Shelf

    The loudest animal sound in the sea: short, metronomic infrasonic pulses. On small speakers you may feel it more than hear it.

  • Reef crackle

    Snapping shrimp · broadband · Moorea Lagoon

    Thousands of shrimp claws collapsing cavitation bubbles. A dense crackle is one of the quickest audible signs of a living reef.

Tip: headphones reveal the fin whale pulses that laptop speakers can't reproduce.

Why we listen

Sound is the ocean's vital sign

Light fades within a hundred metres of the surface, but sound travels for oceans. Acoustics is how whales find each other, how larvae find reefs — and how we can measure ecosystem health, shipping pressure, and climate stress without touching a single animal.

Wavelength exists to make that measurement a public good. When a marine protected area works, the hydrophones hear it first: the chorus thickens, the crackle returns. When it doesn't, the silence is evidence too.

  • For researchers: calibrated archives and detector baselines, citable by DOI.
  • For policymakers: independent noise budgets for shipping lanes and sanctuaries.
  • For everyone: the unedited sound of the sea, free to hear and to keep.
