    Curatorial note

    On keeping what was meant to fade

    An afterimage is not a photograph. It is what the retina invents in the dark immediately after — a stubborn ghost, complementary in colour, already dissolving. Every work gathered here was made by someone who trusted that residue more than the negative.

    Between 1968 and 1999 a loose international company of photographers abandoned the faithful print. They exposed paper to the sun without a camera, dragged images across photocopiers until they decayed, and scanned their own hands on office flatbeds. Galleries had no vitrine for such things, and most of the work was discarded, over-fixed, or simply forgotten in damp boxes.

    The Afterimage Archive does not restore these pictures to some imagined original. We preserve them as they survive — burnt, streaked, half-erased — and hold each one long enough for a viewer to carry its shape away.

    — Editorial desk, Archive 001—947

    How the archive keeps them

    1. 01

      Locate

      Prints surface in estate sales, darkroom clear-outs, and the back drawers of regional camera clubs. Each is logged with whatever provenance survives it.

    2. 02

      Capture, not correct

      Every plate is imaged at its current state. We record the damage; we do not iron it out. The decay is part of the document now.

    3. 03

      Describe

      Process, chemistry, and condition are catalogued so a lost technique can be read even when no maker's name remains attached to it.

    4. 04

      Hold open

      Nothing is deaccessioned. A plate too faint to reproduce still keeps its number and its place in the sequence.
