Reading the rock record

Deep time is not a metaphor. It is the planet’s archive—strata pressed into pages, shells into stone, climates into ice cores. This gallery walks that archive from the Hadean furnace to the Holocene, where our own footprints are only the latest line in a book still being written.

Move through eras, open specimen drawers, and stand inside the great dyings that cleared the stage for whatever came next.

Chronology

A planetary timeline

Seven chapters from fire to forest. Select an era to step into its world.

Hadean — the furnace

Earth accretes from the solar nebula. Impacts keep the surface molten; a moon-forming collision remakes the planet. Oceans may condense late in the eon, but the rock record is almost gone—only zircon crystals whisper of those first waters.

  • Signature: Magma oceans, heavy bombardment
  • Witness: Jack Hills zircons, ~4.4 Ga
  • Life: Not yet written in stone

Archean — first life

Continents begin to stiffen. Stromatolites rise in shallow seas as cyanobacteria invent oxygenic photosynthesis. The atmosphere is still thin on free oxygen, but the biosphere has taken root in mats and microbial reefs.

  • Signature: Greenstone belts, banded iron
  • Witness: Stromatolites of Shark Bay’s deep cousins
  • Life: Prokaryotes, early biofilms

Proterozoic — snowball & oxygen

The Great Oxidation Event remakes chemistry and climate. Supercontinents assemble and break. Earth may freeze pole-to-pole more than once. Near the end, soft-bodied Ediacaran biotas experiment with multicellular form.

  • Signature: Banded iron formations, glacial tillites
  • Witness: Ediacaran impressions, Flinders Ranges
  • Life: Eukaryotes, early animals

Paleozoic — seas & forests

The Cambrian explosion fills oceans with hard parts and eyes. Fish, then tetrapods, claim new niches. Devonian forests and Carboniferous swamps bury carbon that will become coal. The era ends in the greatest dying of all—the end-Permian extinction.

  • Signature: Trilobites, coal measures, reefs
  • Witness: Burgess Shale, Rhynie chert
  • Life: Arthropods to early reptiles

Mesozoic — age of reptiles

Dinosaurs diversify on land; marine reptiles and pterosaurs rule sea and sky. Flowering plants appear mid-era. Pangaea breaks apart, opening new oceans. A bolide impact and volcanism close the book at 66 million years ago.

  • Signature: Dinosaur tracks, chalk seas
  • Witness: Hell Creek, Solnhofen limestone
  • Life: Dinosaurs, early birds & mammals

Cenozoic — mammals & ice

After the K–Pg extinction, mammals radiate into emptied niches. Grasslands spread. Ice ages sculpt continents and coastlines. Hominins emerge in Africa and eventually map the planet they inhabit—including this museum.

  • Signature: Glacial cycles, grasslands
  • Witness: La Brea, Olduvai, Greenland ice
  • Life: Megafauna to modern biota

Anthropocene — our layer

Whether formalized or not, a thin new stratum is forming: plastics, radionuclides, concrete, chicken bones, and a climate spike visible in ice and sediment. Deep Time treats this not as an ending, but as a responsibility written into the rock yet to come.

  • Signature: Technofossils, CO₂ rise
  • Witness: Ice cores, coral bands, city soils
  • Life: Us—and everything we choose next

The collection

Six specimen stories from the drawers—fossils that fixed a moment and made deep time legible.

  • Ordovician · Marine

    Trilobite — Isotelus

    A giant of Paleozoic seas. Segmented armor and compound eyes record an arms race of vision and predation half a billion years old.

  • Carboniferous · Terrestrial

    Lepidodendron bark

    Diamond-patterned scale tree bark from coal swamp forests that fixed carbon into the seams we still mine.

  • Jurassic · Marine

    Ammonite — Dactylioceras

    Spiral chambers of a cephalopod whose coiled shells became the signature icon of deep-time geometry.

  • Cretaceous · Terrestrial

    Hadrosaur tooth battery

    Stacked dental batteries for grinding tough Cretaceous plants—engineering from the last age of non-avian dinosaurs.

  • Archean · Micro

    Stromatolite thin section

    Laminated microbial mats turned to stone—architecture built grain by grain by communities without eyes or bones.

  • Cenozoic · Micro

    Foraminifera tests

    Chambered shells of single-celled ocean dwellers—the fine print of climate history in every deep-sea core.

Atmosphere

Climates of the deep past

Earth’s thermostat has swung from greenhouse hothouses to snowball freezes. These three climate states frame the gallery.

Hothouse Earth

High CO₂, ice-free poles, and warm deep oceans—as in the early Eocene and much of the Mesozoic. Palm pollen near the Arctic, crocodilians at high latitudes.

Proxy: leaf margins · foraminiferal Mg/Ca

Icehouse Earth

Permanent polar ice and glacial–interglacial cycles define the late Cenozoic. Sea level, coastlines, and human migration tracks all shift with the ice.

Proxy: ice cores · oxygen isotopes

Snowball Earth

Neoproterozoic glaciations may have iced the tropics. When the freeze broke, cap carbonates and a reorganized biosphere mark one of life’s greatest resets.

Proxy: tillites · cap carbonates

Turnovers

The five great dyings

Mass extinctions are not footnotes—they are the punctuation that rewrote who inherits the Earth.

  1. Ordovician–Silurian ~445 Ma

    First global wipe

    Glaciation and sea-level fall devastate marine invertebrates. Trilobites and brachiopods take heavy losses before Silurian reefs rebound.

    Severe
  2. Late Devonian ~372 Ma

    Reefs go quiet

    Pulses of anoxia and climate stress collapse reef systems. Armored fish dynasties fade as tetrapods prepare for land.

    Major
  3. End-Permian ~252 Ma

    The Great Dying

    Siberian Traps volcanism and climate chaos erase most marine species and many on land. Recovery takes millions of years; the Mesozoic is born from survivors.

    Catastrophic
  4. End-Triassic ~201 Ma

    Door for dinosaurs

    Central Atlantic Magmatic Province eruptions coincide with marine and terrestrial losses, clearing space for dinosaur dominance.

    Major
  5. Cretaceous–Paleogene ~66 Ma

    Impact winter

    A bolide at Chicxulub, plus Deccan volcanism, ends non-avian dinosaurs and opens the Cenozoic to mammals, birds, and flowering forests.

    Near-total
