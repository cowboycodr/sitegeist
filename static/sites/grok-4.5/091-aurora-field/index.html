The site

Built for darkness

Aurora Field sits above the tree line on a basalt ridge, far from coastal towns and highway glare. Domes, decks, and residences share a single light discipline: nothing brighter than a red lamp after civil twilight.

0.7 m Ritchey–Chrétien

Primary research telescope with corrected field and dual imaging trains for photometry and narrowband work.

All-sky cameras

Continuous wide-field monitoring for aurora morphology, cloud cover, and transient alerts to the control room.

Shielded residences

Blackout rooms, red-path corridors, and workspaces designed so sleep schedules never leak into the sky field.

Open observing deck

Unobstructed horizon for binocular surveys, public programs, and portable setups under full Bortle 1–2 skies.

Research

Residencies under a darker sky

Two- to eight-week stays for observers, instrument builders, and educators who need uninterrupted dark time and a quiet operational culture.

Core program

Observing residency

Queue access on the 0.7 m, night assistant support, and a private reduction desk. Ideal for time-series photometry and survey follow-up.

  • 14–56 nights
  • Shared calibration vault
  • Priority weather windows

Build & test

Instrument residency

Lab bench space, cryogenic support, and after-hours sky tests for cameras, filters, and portable mounts before campaign deployment.

  • Workshop + clean bench
  • Powered pier access
  • Engineering nights

Outreach science

Educator fellowship

Pair curriculum design with live observing. Fellows co-lead one public night and leave with reusable charts and lab modules.

  • 7–21 days
  • Classroom kit library
  • Guest lecture slot

How selection works

Applications open twice yearly. We weight scientific readiness, clear use of dark time, and willingness to keep the site dark for everyone else. No prior polar experience required— just a solid observing plan and respect for the ridge.

Open to all

Public nights

Once the research queue allows, we open the deck and visitor eyepieces. Dress for wind. Bring patience—the sky sets the schedule.

19:30 local

Arrival & light check

Park below the ridge, walk the red path, and stow white lights. Warm drinks in the shelter cabin.

20:15 local

Sky orientation

A short talk on tonight’s planets, aurora chance, and how to use binoculars without spoiling dark adaptation.

21:00 onward

Live observing

Rotating views through the visitor refractor and deck telescopes. If aurora rises, we pause the queue and watch.

23:30 close

Wrap & walk-down

Domes button up, paths re-light in red only, and the last group returns to the lower lot together.

Deep winter

Longest nights, highest aurora odds, and the darkest sky. Sessions may run later when weather holds.

Equinox seasons

Balanced twilight and strong geomagnetic activity. Best for first-time visitors and families.

Late summer

Milder decks and Milky Way cores. Public nights focus on binocular tours and naked-eye landmarks.

Plan ahead

Plan a visit

Aurora Field is intentionally remote. Good visits begin with weather margins, warm layers, and a clear purpose—research, residency, or a public night ticket.

Getting here

  • Coordinates 64.1466° N · ridge access road signed from route 82
  • Travel time About 90 minutes from the regional airfield in clear conditions
  • Seasonal note Winter access requires studded tires or chains; storms can close the ridge overnight
  • Stay options On-site residences for programs; nearby village lodgings for public nights

What to bring

  • Layered clothing rated well below freezing
  • Red flashlight or headlamp (no white LEDs on deck)
  • Binoculars if you have them—loaners are limited
  • Thermos and non-crinkly snacks for long waits
