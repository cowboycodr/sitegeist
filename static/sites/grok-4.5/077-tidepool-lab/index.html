Field station

Where science meets the splash zone

Tidepool Lab sits on a granite headland at 37.7749° N, tracking how heat, harvest, and habitat structure reshape life between high and low water.

What we map each tide cycle

Our team combines continuous sensors with hand-sampled transects so short-lived heat spikes and slow community shifts appear in the same record.

  • Hourly temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen in permanent pools
  • Seasonal biodiversity counts across rocky, kelp, and estuary edges
  • Resilience scores for habitats that buffer heat and wave energy

Temperature shifts

Shallow pools can warm faster than open ocean. We publish pool-to-surface contrasts so managers see which shelves overheat first.

Resilient habitats

Not every shoreline responds the same. We highlight canopy cover, shade geometry, and freshwater input that keep communities stable under stress.

Resilience

Habitats that hold the line

Three shoreline types carry most of the station’s heat and wave load. We score each for structural complexity and recovery after extreme tides.

Rocky intertidal

Layered basalt shelves with deep pools that retain cool water through midday lows. Highest species richness on the transect.

Resilience 82 18 pools

Kelp fringe

Bull kelp and understory algae shade the mid-shore, cutting peak temperatures by up to 2.4°C on calm afternoons.

Resilience 76 Canopy 61%

Estuary mouth

Freshwater seeps and soft sediment create a buffer zone for juveniles, but warm quickly when river flow drops in late summer.
